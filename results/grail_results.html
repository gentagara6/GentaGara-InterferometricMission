ESA SpOC 3 · GECCO 2024 · Interferometric Mission (GRAIL)

GRAIL Results

Satellites around a mothership in an Earth–Moon orbit form one large telescope. Each satellite gets a start position and velocity; the goal is that every baseline (the arrow between two satellites) is different, after 0, 1 and 2 orbits. Fitness is minus the summed fill factor of the XY, XZ and YZ planes at the worst of the three snapshots; lower is better.

The bound assumes every pair gives a new arrow in every plane at every snapshot (n(n−1)+1 arrows out of (2g−1)² per plane). For 40 satellites it is probably not reachable.

Method

Search the positions, calculate the velocities

Random start40 positions near the mothership
Change one satellitenudge 50% · jump 20% · fine-tune velocity 30%
Calculate its velocityleast squares: stay in the same square at t1 and t2
Score itcount distinct arrows directly, 0.9 ms instead of 2.0 ms
Keep or undosimulated annealing: worse moves sometimes kept, less as it cools
Official checkre-scored with the ESA logic, saved if better
Brute force is impossible441 choose 40 ≈ 1.2 × 10⁵⁷ placements, about 10³⁶ ages of the universe at 1 ms each, before even choosing velocities.
The physics is linearPosition at any snapshot = STM × start state. So the 3 velocities can be solved from the 3 positions: 6 targets, 3 unknowns, least squares.
Local search finds the patternOnly one satellite changes per step, so a good pattern isn't destroyed; equal moves let the search wander across flat areas of the score.

Algorithms

Four algorithms, same number of evaluations

Lines: average best fitness over the runs. Shaded: best and worst run. Lower is better.

Calculated velocities matter mostRandom velocities make nearly every satellite drift off the grid, so pure random search stays near 0. Calculating the velocities from the positions turns random guesses into usable solutions; on the tiny instance that alone finds the optimum.
Local search does the restOn 40 satellites, hill climbing and simulated annealing build far better patterns than random guessing. On this short budget hill climbing is ahead, because annealing spends its early steps exploring; in longer runs (100,000 evaluations) the two ended about equal.
Physics

Why the halo instance is harder

Magnitude of the eigenvalues of the one-orbit STM: how much a special start direction is stretched per orbit.

In the DRO every direction keeps its size, so one pattern looks the same at t1 and t2. The halo stretches one direction by 1.19 per orbit and squeezes another by 0.84. The grid's 1.23 inflation covers one orbit of stretching but not two (1.19² = 1.42), and the squeezed direction pushes satellites into shared squares. One pattern can't fit all three snapshots, so the halo scores lower.
