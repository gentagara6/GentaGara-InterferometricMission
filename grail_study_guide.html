GRAIL Study Guide

You choose where a small swarm of satellites starts, and how fast it moves, around a "mothership" flying between Earth and the Moon. Together the satellites work as one giant telescope. The telescope sees most when every pair of satellites is a different arrow apart, and the swarm has to stay that way after one orbit and after two.

Part 1

How the project works, in 8 steps

These are the same steps grail_eval.py runs, in the same order. Each one has a picture.

1

The setting: a mothership in an Earth–Moon orbit

The physics model is called the CR3BP (circular restricted three-body problem). A tiny spacecraft feels the gravity of Earth and Moon. The coordinates rotate with the Earth–Moon line, so in the picture Earth and Moon stay still. In these coordinates the mothership loops around the same path over and over: a DRO (distant retrograde orbit, stable) or a halo orbit (unstable).

2

Your choice: 6 numbers per satellite

For each satellite you pick a start position (dx, dy, dz) and a start velocity (dvx, dvy, dvz), both measured relative to the mothership. All the numbers go into one long list x, grouped by quantity, not by satellite. Here it is for 5 satellites (30 numbers):

3

The physics shortcut: one matrix per snapshot

We don't simulate every satellite. The code simulates the mothership once and builds three 6×6 matrices called STMs (state transition matrices), one for each snapshot time. Any satellite's state later is just matrix × start state, so the same recipe works for every satellite.

4

Three snapshots, and the grid grows a little

Positions are checked at t0 (start), t1 (after 1 orbit) and t2 (after 2 orbits). At t1 and t2 every position is divided by 1.23, which is the same as making the grid 1.23× bigger (1.23 both times, not 1.23²). A satellite with any coordinate outside (−1, 1) is dropped from that snapshot.

5

Snap to the grid

Each position becomes a grid square (on the 11-grid, a number from 0 to 10 per axis). Two satellites in the same square count as one. The real grid is a 3D box of g × g × g cubes. This picture shows just one layer.

6

Three shadows of the 3D box

The 3D box is flattened three ways, like shining a light through it and looking at the shadow: XY (ignore z), XZ (ignore y), YZ (ignore x). Every plane is scored on its own.

7

Count the different arrows (baselines)

In each plane, draw an arrow from every satellite to every other one. A pair gives two arrows (A→B and B→A), and the "zero arrow" (a satellite to itself) also counts once. The code's autocorrelation step does exactly this counting. Only different arrows count, so if two pairs share an arrow, you lose one.

8

Turn it into one score

A g-grid allows (2g−1)² possible arrows (each arrow component goes from −(g−1) to g−1). The fill factor of a plane is the share of those arrows you actually have. Add the three planes at each snapshot, take the worst snapshot, and flip the sign, because the platform minimises.

fill factor = (different arrows) / (2g − 1)²
fitness = − min over {t0, t1, t2} of ( FFXY + FFXZ + FFYZ )

Example, a perfect tiny solution: 21 / 441 = 0.0476 per plane, × 3 planes = 0.1429, so fitness = −0.1429. Lower is better. If one snapshot has everybody off the grid, that snapshot scores 0, and so does the whole solution.

Why "min", not "max"?The website formula says max, but the code uses min. The weakest snapshot sets your score, the way a chain is only as strong as its weakest link.

Part 3

Your progress so far

You've learned

  • What a baseline and a Golomb ruler are.
  • n satellites give n(n−1)/2 pairs.
  • How to work out the best fill factor (you found 1561/1681 yourself).
  • Random solutions almost always score 0, because satellites drift off along y.
  • A satellite at exactly (0,0,0) with zero velocity stays at the centre, and it counts.
  • DRO eigenvalues all have size 1 (stable). The halo has 1.19 and 0.84 (one direction grows).

Lessons from mistakes

  • Check every pair, not just neighbours.
  • The number of baselines is n(n−1)/2, not n.

Watch out

  • cell_to_pos(5, 11) gives 0.0909, not 0. A satellite at "square 5" is not exactly at the mothership, so it can drift.
  • The scoring box is 3D (g × g × g), even though each plane is 2D.

3 · Why do satellites drift along y, but not along x or z?

Done
The answerAn x offset puts the satellite in a different lane, so its lap time is slightly different and it falls further behind (or gets further ahead) every orbit. A y offset keeps the same lane and lap time, so it only swings. A z offset keeps the same lane and lap time, so it only bobs up and down. The mothership travels along y, so "ahead or behind on the track" shows up as y: that's why the drift is in y.

4 · 6 numbers in, 9 numbers out: what does linear algebra say?

Done
Your resultAt t0 the satellite is at its start position (STM₀ = identity), so the 3 position knobs set t0 directly. That leaves 3 velocity knobs for 6 targets at t1 and t2: an overdetermined system, so usually no exact solution, only a best compromise (least squares) that keeps positions inside the grid. Example: y at t1 wants dvy ≈ 0.69, y at t2 wants ≈ 0.78; the good range 0.71–0.80 is the compromise. Smarter search: search the 3 positions, calculate the 3 velocities.
What an STM is, simplyA recipe card. For each later coordinate, it says how much of each of your 6 start numbers to mix in. Real example from your code (after ÷ 1.23): y after 2 orbits = 38.84 × dx − 15.01 × dvy (+ tiny bits of the others). Your Question 5 rule is this recipe set to 0: 38.84 / 15.01 ≈ 2.6, so vy ≈ 2.6 × x.

Simple version: three small questions.

  1. t0: nothing has moved yet. Where is the satellite at t0? So how many of the 9 outputs do your 3 start positions set directly?
  2. t1 and t2: that leaves 3 velocity "knobs" for 6 outputs. In Question 5, 1 knob (vy) fixed t2 but not t1. With 3 knobs and 6 targets, can you usually hit all 6 exactly?
  3. The trick: you found vy = 2.6 × x by solving one equation, not by guessing. How could that help a search with 40 satellites?
Hint 1

Look at make_stms: the integration starts from np.eye(6).

Hint 2 (bigger hint)

Once the start position is fixed, the later positions depend linearly on the 3 velocity numbers. A linear system can be solved, or solved "as close as possible" (least squares, np.linalg.lstsq), instead of guessed.
